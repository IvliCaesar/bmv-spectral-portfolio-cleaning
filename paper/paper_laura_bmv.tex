\begin{abstract}
Does covariance estimation materially change minimum-variance portfolio
outcomes in Mexican equities once long-only constraints and periodic
rebalancing are considered? We compare sample, eigenvalue-clipped,
Ledoit--Wolf, and rotationally invariant (RIE) covariance estimators in a
37-stock Bolsa Mexicana de Valores panel. Daily gross total returns provide
3,706 observations; portfolio comparisons use simple returns, a common
chronological holdout, long-only portfolios, an equally weighted benchmark,
and a 504-observation rolling window with 21-observation buy-and-hold periods.
In the fixed holdout, unconstrained RIE reduces daily volatility by 0.0046
percentage points relative to the sample covariance estimator, but its
annualized Sharpe difference is $-0.025$ (20-day moving-block 95\% interval
$[-0.045,-0.004]$; HAC $p=0.015$). Under long-only constraints, RIE's Sharpe
is $-0.035$, compared with $0.450$ for $1/N$. In the rolling design,
long-only $1/N$ attains a Sharpe of $0.767$, while RIE attains $-0.646$;
RIE's Sharpe difference relative to $1/N$ is $-1.413$ (21-day block-bootstrap
95\% interval $[-2.333,-0.641]$). Hypothetical proportional-cost scenarios
do not reverse this ranking, but no transaction costs are directly observed.
The results do not establish a generalizable estimator ranking. They show
that investor-relevant constraints, rebalancing, and comparison with a naive
benchmark materially change the assessment of covariance cleaning in this
selected Mexican-equity panel.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} emerging markets; Mexican equities; portfolio
selection; covariance estimation; minimum-variance portfolio; transaction
costs; out-of-sample evaluation.

\section{Introduction}

Covariance estimates are central to minimum-variance portfolio construction:
the optimizer inverts the estimated risk matrix, so sampling error in small
eigenvalues can lead to large and unstable positions. Spectral cleaning and
shrinkage regularize this input, but a lower estimated portfolio variance
does not ensure superior realized risk-adjusted returns or a feasible
investment strategy. The comparison is especially relevant in emerging
equity markets, where investable universes can be concentrated and trading
frictions and data coverage can be important to portfolio design
\cite{bekaert2007}.

The empirical question is \emph{how sensitive are minimum-variance portfolio
outcomes to covariance estimation when Mexican equities are evaluated under
long-only constraints and periodic rebalancing?} We compare four estimators
on one common daily total-return panel, include the equally weighted $1/N$
portfolio, and distinguish a static-weight holdout from a rolling
buy-and-hold protocol. The application is a single-country analysis. It
speaks to the choice of performance benchmarks and the limitations of
covariance-based allocation in a Mexican equity sample; it does not estimate
a regional emerging-market effect or a causal institutional mechanism.

Three results motivate this distinction. First, in the fixed holdout, RIE
has lower realized volatility than the sample covariance estimator but a
lower Sharpe ratio. Second, the simple $1/N$ portfolio has a substantially
higher point estimate of Sharpe than the long-only covariance-based
portfolios. Third, monthly rolling comparisons preserve the large point
estimate gap between $1/N$ and RIE, while estimated turnover and assumed
cost scenarios are reported explicitly. The analysis is deliberately
conditional on a retrospectively assembled 37-stock universe and one
historical path.

\section{Data and methods}

\subsection{Daily total-return panel}

The panel contains 37 Mexican equities mapped one-to-one from the original
38-security candidate list to issuer tabs in the Bloomberg workbook
downloaded in September 2026. We use Bloomberg's
\texttt{TOT\_RETURN\_INDEX\_GROSS\_DVDS} field rather than unadjusted
\texttt{PX\_LAST} prices. The 37 mapped tickers are MFRISCOA-1, RA, SIMECB,
ICHB, GENTERA, SORIANAB, ELEKTRA, CHDRAUIB, LIVEPOLC-1, AUTLANB, HERDEZ,
GAPB, OMAB, MEGACPO, PINFRA, LABB, ALSEA, ARA, ASURB, AMXB, AC, AXTELCPO,
KIMBERA, CEMEXCPO, BIMBOA, BOLSAA, GCARSOA1, FEMSAUBD, GFNORTEO, GFINBURO,
ORBIA, GRUMAB, GMEXICOB, PE\&OLES, TLEVISACPO, SIGMAFA, and WALMEX. FUNO11
was absent from the workbook and was excluded rather than replaced. The
balanced panel runs from 18 July 2011 through 29 September 2025 and contains
3,706 daily log returns with no gaps longer than seven calendar days.

The last 20\% of observations (741 dates, 28 November 2022--29 September
2025) form a chronological holdout. Covariance matrices are estimated on the
preceding 2,965 dates, so $q=N/T_{\mathrm{train}}=37/2965\approx0.0125$.
The security set is retrospective, not reconstructed from historical
BMV/BIVA membership. Its balanced coverage therefore does not remove
survivorship, ticker-mapping, or ex-post selection bias. The 37 names are
not treated as a representative index or as a complete investable universe.

\subsection{Covariance estimators and portfolios}

We compare the sample covariance (SM), Mar\v cenko--Pastur eigenvalue
clipping (CL), Ledoit--Wolf identity-target linear shrinkage (LW), and
analytic rotationally invariant nonlinear shrinkage (RIE). CL standardizes
the covariance to a correlation matrix, retains eigenvalues above the
Mar\v cenko--Pastur upper edge, replaces the remaining bulk eigenvalues by
their average, and restores the original marginal scales. RIE applies
kernel-based analytic shrinkage to sample eigenvalues while retaining sample
eigenvectors \cite{laloux1999,ledoitwolf2004,ledoit2022}. The implementation
is designed for $q<1$.

For each covariance estimate $\widehat\Sigma$, the unconstrained
global minimum-variance weights are
\[
  \widehat w =
  \frac{\widehat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}
       {\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\widehat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}},
  \qquad \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\widehat w=1.
\]
The long-only version minimizes $w^{\mathsf T}\widehat\Sigma w$ subject to
$\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}w=1$ and $0\leq w_i\leq1$. We also evaluate the
equally weighted portfolio $w_i=1/N$, a standard benchmark in portfolio
selection \cite{demiguel2009}.

For each asset, $\ell_{i,t}$ denotes the log change in its gross total-return
index; the simple return is $r_{i,t}=\exp(\ell_{i,t})-1$. In the static
holdout, covariance estimates and target weights are fit once on the
training sample, and the portfolio is restored to its target weights daily:
$r_{p,t}=\sum_iw_i r_{i,t}$. This convention is distinct from holding shares
fixed throughout the test period.

The rolling sensitivity estimates each covariance matrix from the preceding
504 observations, sets weights every 21 observations, and holds shares fixed
within each 21-observation block. Portfolio wealth is reconstructed from
asset-price relatives within each block. Twenty-five non-overlapping blocks
cover 26 September 2023--29 September 2025. This rolling analysis shares its
test observations with the fixed holdout and is a protocol sensitivity, not
an independent replication.

\subsection{Performance, turnover, and inference}

We report annualized arithmetic mean return $252\bar r_p$, annualized
volatility $\sqrt{252}s_p$, and annualized Sharpe
$\sqrt{252}(\bar r_p-r_f/252)/s_p$, using a fixed 8\% annual risk-free rate.
Turnover is one-way traded notional divided by pre-trade portfolio value.
For the static protocol, daily turnover measures the trade needed to restore
target weights after market drift; in the rolling design, turnover is
measured at each rebalance.

No transaction-cost observations are available. We therefore subtract
hypothetical proportional costs of 0, 5, 10, 25, and 50 basis points per
unit of one-way traded notional. Initial deployment costs, taxes,
market impact, liquidity limits, and security-specific bid--ask spreads are
not estimated. The cost scenarios are sensitivities, not measurements of
Mexican implementation costs.

Paired moving-block bootstrap intervals use 20,000 replications and common
resampled date indices (10-, 20-, and 42-observation blocks for the static
holdout; 10-, 21-, and 42-observation blocks for the rolling comparison).
HAC standard errors with 20 lags provide two-sided tests for Sharpe and
volatility differences. Inference is conditional on the fitted weights and
chosen universe and does not propagate covariance-estimation, universe
selection, or model-selection uncertainty. The fixed-holdout RIE--SM
comparison is the primary inferential contrast; the rolling and $1/N$
comparisons are sensitivity analyses. We do not adjust for the number of
secondary comparisons.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Fixed chronological holdout}

Table~\ref{tab:primary} reports the four unconstrained portfolios. RIE has
lower holdout volatility than SM (0.6952\% versus 0.6998\% per day), but its
Sharpe is lower ($-0.232$ versus $-0.207$). Ledoit--Wolf has both the lowest
volatility and the highest Sharpe among covariance-based estimators. The
ranking therefore depends on the loss function; a volatility reduction by
itself is not evidence of improved investor outcomes.

The RIE-minus-SM daily volatility difference is $-0.00462$ percentage points
(20-observation block-bootstrap 95\% interval $[-0.00713,-0.00113]$; HAC
$p=0.0036$). The Sharpe difference is $-0.0248$ (95\% interval
$[-0.0451,-0.0038]$; HAC $p=0.015$). The Sharpe interval remains below zero
at block lengths 10 and 42. Under long-only constraints, RIE's Sharpe
difference from SM is $-0.0113$ (20-observation interval
$[-0.0242,0.0028]$; HAC $p=0.086$), while its volatility difference remains
negative. These intervals are conditional on weights fitted in the training
sample.

RIE's unconstrained effective number of assets is 10.85 compared with 10.58
for SM; Shannon effective breadth is 25.91 versus 25.37. Both measures are
far below the 37 assets of the equal-weight portfolio. These statistics
describe portfolio weights and should not be interpreted as market-wide
investor diversity.

\subsection{Long-only portfolios and the naive benchmark}

The long-only results sharpen the applied comparison. On the fixed holdout,
$1/N$ has a Sharpe of 0.450 and an annualized arithmetic mean return of
13.72\%, compared with $-0.035$ and 7.62\% for RIE. The $1/N$ portfolio also
has higher volatility (12.72\% versus 11.10\%). The paired RIE-minus-$1/N$
Sharpe estimate is $-0.485$, but its interval is wide
($[-1.068,0.053]$; HAC $p=0.099$); the point-estimate ranking is not a
precise estimate of expected performance beyond this sample.

\subsection{Rolling deployment and cost sensitivity}

The 25-block rolling comparison does not establish a performance advantage
for RIE over SM. For long-only portfolios, annualized Sharpe is $-0.646$ for
RIE and $-0.736$ for SM; their difference of $+0.090$ has a 21-observation
block-bootstrap 95\% interval $[-0.052,0.185]$ and HAC $p=0.176$. The
long-only $1/N$ portfolio attains a Sharpe of 0.767 and a mean return of
18.19\%. Its Sharpe difference from RIE is $1.413$ (RIE minus $1/N$ is
$-1.413$; 95\% interval $[-2.333,-0.641]$; HAC $p=0.0013$). This contrast
is exploratory because it uses the same test history and one set of model
choices.

Mean one-way turnover per actual rebalance is 5.05\% for long-only RIE and
2.81\% for $1/N$. At the assumed upper cost of 50 basis points per one-way
turnover, annualized mean return is 0.57\% for RIE and 18.03\% for $1/N$;
at 10 basis points, the corresponding estimates are 0.80\% and 18.16\%.
These differences do not establish expected future returns: the assumed
costs are not transaction data, and initial portfolio formation costs are
excluded.

\section{Discussion}

The central empirical result is not that one covariance estimator dominates.
In the fixed holdout, RIE achieves slightly lower volatility but a lower
Sharpe than the sample estimator; Ledoit--Wolf performs better on both
reported risk and Sharpe point estimates among the covariance-based
unconstrained portfolios. Long-only estimation reduces reliance on short
positions but does not make covariance optimization outperform the naive
benchmark in this sample. The rolling analysis reaches the same practical
warning: point-in-time estimator rankings and implementation rules matter.

For emerging-market investment research, this distinction is material.
Performance claims based only on estimated or realized minimum variance can
omit the benchmark an allocator could actually hold and the turnover needed
to maintain optimized weights. Our cost grid demonstrates how to report
such sensitivities, but it does not establish local execution costs,
investability, or a causal effect of market institutions. Those questions
require quote-level spreads, trading volumes, market impact, a local
risk-free series, and institutional portfolio constraints.

Several limitations remain substantive. The 37 names were mapped from a
retrospective security list, not a point-in-time BMV/BIVA membership
database; survivor and selection bias cannot be ruled out. One 20\% holdout
and one overlapping rolling sensitivity are not independent replications.
Bootstrap and HAC inference condition on weights and the selected universe.
The cost exercise uses assumed basis-point rates rather than observed
execution prices and omits liquidity limits, market impact, short-sale
frictions, taxes, and initial deployment. The risk-free rate is fixed at
8\% rather than matched to a local yield series. These limitations prevent
claims about an investable strategy, market-wide diversification, or
generalization to other emerging economies.

\section{Conclusion}

For a daily gross-total-return panel of 37 Mexican equities, spectral
covariance cleaning changes the estimated portfolio comparison but does not
yield a consistent Sharpe advantage. RIE lowers fixed-holdout volatility
relative to SM while its Sharpe is lower; under long-only constraints, $1/N$
has the highest Sharpe point estimate. The same benchmark remains ahead in
the rolling analysis and across the stated assumed-cost scenarios. The
evidence supports a practical conclusion for this sample: covariance
cleaning should be assessed jointly with simple benchmarks, portfolio
constraints, turnover, and the timing of rebalancing.

The next research step is a multi-market, point-in-time panel of emerging
equities with delisted securities, consistent total-return histories, and
institution-specific trading-cost data. Such a design could test whether
the benchmark and estimator patterns documented here extend beyond this
selected Mexican panel and whether they have implications for local
investment institutions.
